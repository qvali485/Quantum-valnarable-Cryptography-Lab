ECC & ECDH Key Exchange, Step by Step

Elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman (ECDH) lets two people agree on a shared secret over a public channel. It is the common way modern protocols (e.g. TLS 1.3, secure messaging) establish keys. This is a tiny teaching curve; real curves like P-256 use numbers about 77 digits long. Drag the sliders.

The curve: y² = x³ + 2x + 2 (mod 17). “Mod 17” means we only keep the remainder after dividing by 17, like a clock with 17 hours. Division by k means multiplying by k’s inverse on that clock. Base point G = (5, 1); adding G to itself 19 times returns to the start (“∞”).

Dots = every point on the curve. G base, A Alice’s public point, B Bob’s public point, S the shared secret.

Attacker who can solve the discrete log

An eavesdropper sees G, A and B. To get the secret they must find a from A = a·G. On this toy curve that’s trial and error.

What If an Attacker Had a Cryptographically Relevant Quantum Computer?

  1. The attacker obtains an RSA public key: modulus N and exponent e.
  2. They run a sufficiently capable implementation of Shor’s algorithm to factor N.
  3. The factors let them compute the private-key parameters, under the usual RSA key-generation assumptions.
  4. They may then decrypt captured RSA-encrypted key material or forge RSA signatures, depending on protocol, available data and key usage.

Encryption / key transport: captured ciphertexts can be decrypted later (“harvest now, decrypt later”) once the private key is recovered.

Signatures: a recovered key lets an attacker forge signatures going forward; old signatures aren’t “decrypted.”

A public key alone does not reveal messages. Practical attacks depend on the protocol, captured traffic, and whether the private key is recovered.

Reality check: today’s classical computers and small quantum simulators cannot factor real-world RSA moduli using Shor’s algorithm. Nothing here implies arbitrary RSA-2048 keys can be broken with current quantum hardware.

Why Post-Quantum Cryptography Matters

Shor’s algorithm threatens RSA and conventional elliptic-curve cryptography because both rely on problems (factoring, discrete logarithms) that a sufficiently powerful fault-tolerant quantum computer could solve efficiently.

AES is different. Grover’s algorithm gives only a quadratic speedup for idealized unstructured key search—not the polynomial-time break Shor gives against RSA. Larger symmetric keys keep comfortable margins.

Migration is a program, not a swap: cryptographic inventory, algorithm selection, protocol compatibility, testing, and cryptographic agility so you can change algorithms again.

Shor’s algorithm demonstrates why sufficiently powerful quantum computers could threaten RSA and conventional elliptic-curve cryptography. The threat is not that quantum computers magically guess a private key; it is that quantum period-finding can enable efficient integer factorization.
