Security relies on the practical difficulty of recovering p and q from a sufficiently large n, when the key is generated correctly.

Knowing n = 391 and recovering its prime factors gives back 17 and 23.

Toy example only. Real RSA moduli are hundreds or thousands of bits long, and the security of actual RSA depends on more than modulus size (padding, key generation, protocol design).

RSA isn’t broken by “finding a prediction.” The relevant mathematical weakness is integer factorization: an attacker who can factor a sufficiently large modulus can undermine RSA’s security.

    A Simplified Shor Circuit

    Simplified conceptual circuit — not a working Qiskit implementation. Hover or tap a block.

    In one sentence: the quantum part only finds the loop length r, very fast. Everything else is school-level maths.

    1 · Setup: two notepads

    Think of two notepads. The top one will ask lots of questions at once. The bottom one starts at 1 and will hold the answers.

    2 · Hadamard gates: ask every question at once

    A normal computer tries x = 1, then 2, then 3… Here the top notepad holds all the x values at the same time, like a spinning coin that is not yet heads or tails.

    3 · Modular exponentiation: find the loop

    For every x at once, work out 2ˣ mod N. Because we keep only remainders, the answers go round in a loop, like hours on a clock. How long the loop is (called r) is the secret we want.

    4 · QFT: find the beat

    Like a music app that listens to a song and tells you its beat. The loop makes a repeating pattern, and the QFT turns that pattern into a few sharp spikes so the loop length stands out.

    5 · Measurement: take a snapshot

    Looking at the qubits forces one answer. It is picked by chance, but it very likely lands near a spike. One snapshot gives a hint about r, not the full answer.

    6 · Normal computer finishes the job

    A classical computer turns the hint into r with a fraction trick (continued fractions), then takes two gcds to get the factors, for example 17 and 23. If it fails, try again with a different guess.

    Understand the maths — 3 plain steps (N = 391)

    Step 1 · Guess. Pick a = 2 (any number sharing no factor with 391).

    Step 2 · Find the repeat length r. Keep multiplying by 2 and keep only the remainder after dividing by 391: 2, 4, 8, 16 … Eventually you land back on 1. The number of steps taken is r. For 391, r = 88. This is the only hard part, and the part a quantum computer speeds up.

    Step 3 · Turn r into factors. Go half-way: 2⁴⁴ mod 391 = 254. Now take two gcds (the biggest number dividing both): gcd(254 − 1, 391) = gcd(253, 391) = 23 and gcd(254 + 1, 391) = gcd(255, 391) = 17. Check: 17 × 23 = 391 ✓

    When it fails. A repeat length alone isn’t enough. If r is odd, or the half-way value is 390 (that is, −1), both gcds come out as 1 or 391. Then just try another guess a.

    Where the QFT fits. The quantum computer’s measurement gives a blurry fraction close to k/r. A classical trick (continued fractions) reads r off that fraction. Everything after the measurement is ordinary classical maths.
